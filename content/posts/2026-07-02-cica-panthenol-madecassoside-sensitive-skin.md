**A cream can advertise cica or a high panthenol percentage and still be a poor match for your skin.** Before buying a large Korean skincare set, compare the exact formula, the quantity you will use, and the evidence behind its claims. An ingredient name does not establish that a finished product treats redness, acne, or eczema.

This is a label-reading and shopping guide, not a diagnosis or a product trial. Sources were checked on October 3, 2026.

## Three names to locate on the box

| Front-label term | What to look for in the actual ingredient list | What the name cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Cica | The specific Centella asiatica ingredient, if present | That every product using the marketing term has the same composition |
| Madecassoside | Madecassoside listed separately | That a cream is more effective than another Centella formula |
| Panthenol / B5 | Panthenol and any percentage the manufacturer explicitly supplies | That a higher percentage is automatically more suitable |

For a real label example, the US [Cicaplast Balm B5+ page](https://www.laroche-posay.us/our-products/body/body-lotion/cicaplast-balm-b5-for-dry-skin-irritations-3606000437449.html) lists panthenol, Centella asiatica leaf extract and madecassoside together. That confirms ingredients in that listed version; it is not a comparative test or a recommendation to use it for a skin condition. Check the package sold in your country because versions can differ.

## Do ingredient studies prove a shop product works?

A [2025 randomized study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40613435/) investigated a panthenol-enriched mask containing other ingredients after specific laser treatments. It did not test every cica cream on a retail shelf. A result for a combination formula in that setting cannot isolate one ingredient or establish that a different product will work for your symptoms.

When a shop description says “clinically tested,” ask what was tested: the finished formula or one ingredient; hydration or a disease outcome; the same formula and users or a different group. If those details are absent, leave the claim out of your comparison rather than turning it into a reason to pay more.

## A two-product comparison you can use in the aisle

Write down these fields for both candidates before choosing:

1. **Exact name and version:** include cream versus balm, size and regional packaging.
2. **Your known reactions:** compare the whole ingredient list with products you already tolerate or avoid.
3. **Evidence:** distinguish the manufacturer's description from independent testing of that product.
4. **Amount:** count only the same formula you intend to use; a bonus cleanser is not extra cream.
5. **Trial size:** compare the cost of a small first purchase with the potential waste of an unsuitable large set.
6. **Use instructions:** save the label and stop-use instructions; do not invent a regimen from a percentage alone.

For products sold in the US, the [FDA's labeling summary](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-labeling-regulations/summary-cosmetics-labeling-requirements) explains ingredient-order exceptions, including ingredients at 1% or less. You cannot calculate a concentration just from its position. This US rule is not a statement about every Korean domestic label.

## What a lower unit price can hide

Suppose, hypothetically, a 40 ml tube costs ₩16,000 and a 100 ml tube costs ₩30,000. They cost ₩400/ml and ₩300/ml respectively. The larger tube has a lower unit price, but costs ₩14,000 more upfront. If it is your first purchase and you cannot use it comfortably, the cheaper unit price provides no practical saving. These are calculation examples, not current store quotes.

The FDA also cautions that [“hypoallergenic” is not an allergy-free guarantee](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-labeling-claims/hypoallergenic-cosmetics). The [American Academy of Dermatology's home product-testing guide](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products/) describes a small-area test over seven to ten days. Stop if a reaction develops; home testing does not replace professional evaluation of persistent or severe symptoms.

If your skin is painful, swollen or persistently inflamed, seek medical advice instead of buying another “soothing” layer. Do not stop prescribed treatment based on a shopping guide.
